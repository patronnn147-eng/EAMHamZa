General Introduction

Industrial maintenance is a central concern for any production company: an unanticipated
failure on a manufacturing line leads to costly downtime, quality loss, and risks to operator
safety. Traditional asset management approaches (EAM -- Enterprise Asset Management),
based on corrective maintenance or preventive maintenance planned at fixed intervals, have
reached their limits: they do not leverage machine operating data to anticipate actual failures.

   It is within this context that the end-of-studies project carried out at SagemCom takes
place: the design and implementation of an EAM platform integrating predictive mainte-
nance capabilities through Machine Learning, intelligent field support for technicians, and a
document-based conversational assistant.

   The problem addressed is the following: how can a single platform unify traditional indus-
trial asset management (users, machines, work orders and intervention orders) with failure
prediction capabilities and intelligent support for maintenance teams?

   This report is structured into five chapters. The first chapter presents the general context
of the internship, the host company, and the problem statement. The second chapter covers
requirements analysis and specification. The third chapter details the system design. The
fourth chapter describes the implementation carried out. The fifth chapter presents the
tests performed and the results obtained. The report closes with a general conclusion that
addresses the problem statement and outlines avenues for improvement.

1 General Project Context

1.1 Presentation of the host company

SagemCom is an industrial group specializing in the design and manufacturing of high-value
communication equipment (set-top boxes, internet boxes, smart meters). The company oper-
ates automated production lines combining electronic component assembly (SMT), functional
testing, and quality control, spread across several production areas.

1.1.1 Presentation of the host department
The internship took place within the Software Engineering and Innovation Department,
whose main mission is to develop internal digital tools aimed at modernizing the management
and maintenance of production equipment, drawing on the industrial data generated by the
manufacturing lines.

1.2 Project context

The management of industrial assets (production machines, assembly lines, test benches)
traditionally relies on preventive maintenance cycles planned at fixed intervals, regardless of
the actual condition of the equipment. This approach leads either to unnecessary interven-
tions on machines still in good condition, or to unanticipated failures on machines degrading
faster than expected.

   The rise of industrial sensors and machine learning techniques now makes it possible to
consider predictive maintenance: estimating the probability of failure, the likely failure type,
the remaining useful life (RUL) and detecting abnormal machine behavior from its operating
data.

1.3 Problem statement

The internship's problem statement is to design a single platform that covers at once:
   · the classic operational management of an EAM system (users, machines, work orders,
       intervention orders, scheduling);

                                                          2
   · predictive maintenance modules based on machine learning (failure probability, failure
       type classification, remaining useful life estimation, anomaly detection, intervention
       prioritization and scheduling, spare parts coordination);

   · intelligent support for field teams: contextualized alerts, prediction explainability ("why
       this alert?"), and a conversational assistant able to answer technicians' questions based
       on maintenance documentation and the real-time health status of the machines.

1.4 Internship objectives

The objectives assigned for this internship are:

   · design and implement the backend and frontend of the EAM platform;

   · design and train the predictive maintenance models (P1 to P7);

   · integrate a RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) service for documentary assistance,
       connected to real-time ML predictions;

   · validate the whole through functional tests and a performance evaluation of the models.

1.5 Working methodology

Development followed an iterative approach: each feature was the subject of a written spec-
ification (requirements document), followed by a detailed implementation plan, before being
built and verified. This methodology made it possible to maintain full traceability of design
decisions (technical choices, alternatives considered, justifications) throughout the six months
of the internship.

   For the six initially scoped Machine Learning problems specifically, this iterative approach
took the concrete form of a structured, repeatable eight-step pipeline applied to each problem
in turn, described below together with the original problem definitions that preceded any
implementation work.

1.5.2 Eight-step model development pipeline

Each of the six initial problems (binary failure prediction, failure-type classification, remaining-
useful-life estimation, anomaly detection, work-order priority, and maintenance scheduling)
was carried through the same eight stages, summarized in Table 1.3.

   At the data-preparation stage (step 3), a first, self-imposed warning was already recorded
for the failure-prediction problem: the five failure sub-labels (TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF, RNF)
are deterministic sub-causes of the overall failure flag and must not be used as input features,
on pain of artificially inflating accuracy. This is the same class of issue (input features
encoding the target) that the later, systematic label-leakage audit formally confirmed and
tagged as validation_status: "leaked" in production (Section 5.2.2); it had already
been anticipated qualitatively at this earlier stage of the project, but not yet proven or acted
upon.

   Because the failure class represents only 3.39% of the benchmark dataset, step 4 applied
stratified splitting together with SMOTE oversampling (synthetic minority over-sampling,
applied strictly after the split to avoid leaking test information into the synthetic samples) for
the classification problems, while the maintenance-scheduling problem (being a time series)
was split chronologically, since a random shuffle would let the model train on data from after
the point it is tested on.

   Table 1.4 summarizes the model initially selected for each problem at step 5, and the
evaluation target fixed for it at step 7.

   Step 8 (tune and improve) defined, for every problem, a concrete lever to close the gap
between the measured and the target metric: hyperparameter search via GridSearchCV,
classification-threshold adjustment to trade precision for recall, alternative resampling strate-
gies (SMOTE, ADASYN, or undersampling), targeted feature engineering (for instance, a

                                                                                                                     6
temperature-delta feature to strengthen the heat-dissipation-failure signal), class-weighting
of rare priority levels, and early stopping to prevent overfitting on the boosted-tree models.

   Several of these initial choices were revised during productionisation, once the label-leakage
and validation-methodology issues described in Chapter 5 were identified: Random Forest
gave way to XGBoost with group-holdout validation for P1, and the scheduling problem was
reworked away from a pure Prophet time-series forecast towards the classifier-based approach
and the broader Wave 2 signal-fusion layer presented in Chapter 3. A seventh problem, spare-
parts demand forecasting (P7), was added beyond this original six-problem scope once the
platform's data model matured enough to support it.

2.2 Functional requirements

2.2.1 Authentication and access management
The system must require authentication with a username and password before granting any
access to the platform. Each user is assigned a single role (Administrator, Operations Man-
ager, Head Technician, Technician) that determines the full set of accessible features and
data. Any attempt to access an unauthorized resource must be rejected by the server, inde-
pendently of any interface-side control.

2.2.2 User and role management
The administrator must be able to create, edit, deactivate, and delete user accounts, and
assign them a role. The system must guarantee that a deactivated account can no longer
authenticate, without deleting its associated action history.

2.2.3 Machine asset management
The system must allow machines to be registered with their technical characteristics (identi-
fier, category, location, commissioning date). Each machine has a detailed, viewable record
combining its current health status, maintenance history, active alerts, and ongoing predic-
tions. The administrator can add, edit, or archive a machine without affecting the existing
work orders attached to it.

2.2.4 Machine labeling
The Operations Manager must be able to attach descriptive labels to each machine to fa-
cilitate classification and search. The system must support creating, editing, and deleting
labels, as well as assigning them to several machines at once.

2.2.5 Work order management
The system must support creating a work order (OT) by specifying the machine concerned,
the nature of the intervention, the priority, and the desired date. A work order follows a
defined lifecycle: pending, assigned, in progress, completed, or cancelled. Every transition
must be logged with the identity of the user and a timestamp. The Head Technician can view
all work orders, filter them by status or priority, and reassign them in the event a technician
is unavailable.

2.2.6 Intervention order management

An intervention order (BT) is created from a work order and assigned to a specific technician
with a time slot. The technician must be able to view their assigned intervention orders,
record the actions performed and the parts replaced, and close the intervention. When an
intervention is closed, the system must automatically trigger an update of the machine's
health score and a retroactive evaluation of earlier predictions.

2.2.7 Intervention scheduling

The system must provide a calendar view of planned interventions, navigable by day, week,
and month. The Head Technician must be able to schedule a new intervention, modify an
existing time slot, and check technician availability before any assignment. The schedule
must visually flag assignment conflicts (a technician already booked for the same time slot).

2.2.8 Telemetry data collection and monitoring

The system must record sensor readings for each machine: air temperature, process tem-
perature, rotational speed, torque, and tool wear. This data constitutes the primary source
for the prediction models and must be viewable as a timestamped history per machine. In
the absence of real telemetry data for a machine, the system must explicitly report the
unavailability of predictions rather than substituting default values.

2.2.9 Machine learning predictions

For each machine with telemetry data available, the system must expose six predictive indi-
cators computed by machine learning models:

   · Failure probability (P1): estimate of short-term failure risk;

   · Likely failure type (P2): classification among known failure modes;

   · Remaining useful life -- RUL (P3): estimate in days until the next required
       intervention, together with a confidence interval;

   · Behavioral anomaly score (P4): detection of abnormal sensor behavior via an
       ensemble of four methods (Isolation Forest, Z-score, cluster deviation, autoencoder);

   · Processing priority (P5): recommended priority level for the associated work order;

   · Maintenance schedule (P6): proposed optimal date for the next preventive inter-
       vention.

Each prediction must be accompanied by a plain-language explanation, free of technical
jargon, accessible to a non-specialist user.

                                                                                                                    14
2.2.10 Alerts and notifications
The system must automatically generate alerts when a model detects a high failure risk, a
behavioral anomaly, or a foreseeable parts shortage. Alerts must be classified by criticality
level and viewable by the Head Technician, with filtering by priority. An alert that has
already been handled (linked to a closed work order) must be automatically archived and no
longer appear in the active list.

2.2.11 Spare parts coordination
Based on failure predictions, the system must estimate future spare parts needs per machine
and generate provisioning proposals. These proposals take the form of draft purchase orders
subject to explicit human validation: no order can be issued automatically without approval
from the Head Technician. The system also exposes an operational readiness score per
machine, combining health status, stock level, and proximity to the next intervention.

2.2.12 Knowledge base and conversational assistant
The administrator must be able to import maintenance documents (manuals, technical data
sheets, procedures) into the platform's knowledge base. The system must allow any authen-
ticated user to query this knowledge base in natural language via a conversational assistant.
The assistant's responses must combine information extracted from relevant documents with
the real-time health status of the machine in question, in order to provide contextualized
advice to the technician in the field.

2.2.13 Dashboards and reports
Each role has a personalized dashboard: a fleet-wide overview for the Administrator and the
Operations Manager, an intervention and alert monitoring view for the Head Technician,
and an assigned-tasks view for the Technician. The system must support viewing summary
reports on maintenance activity, machine performance, and the evolution of predictive indi-
cators.

2.2.14 Archiving and traceability
Every action performed on a work order, an intervention order, or an alert must be logged in
an audit trail with the identity of the author, the date, and the nature of the action. Closed
interventions and handled alerts must be preserved in a viewable, non-modifiable history,
serving as the basis for the continuous evaluation of the predictive models.

2.3.3 Performance

The response time for common operations (viewing a machine record, listing work orders,

learning model inference, which involves several computation pipelines, must complete in
under five seconds for all six indicators of a machine. The interface must remain responsive
during asynchronous calls to the ML microservice, displaying a loading indicator rather than
blocking the user.

2.3.4 Availability and resilience

Classic management features -- work orders, intervention orders, scheduling -- must remain
fully operational even if the Machine Learning microservice is unavailable. If telemetry data
is missing for a machine, the system must explicitly report it and provide a health score
computed from maintenance history, without substituting fictitious values. The platform's
various services (backend, ML microservice, RAG service, object storage) must be deployed
as independent containers, allowing any one of them to be restarted or updated without
interrupting the others.

2.3.5 Maintainability and extensibility

The architecture must clearly separate responsibilities between the presentation, business
logic, and data access layers, to facilitate adding new predictive models or new roles without
a full overhaul. Database migrations must be versioned and applied incrementally through
a dedicated tool (Alembic), ensuring traceability of schema evolution. The ML model code

                                                                                                                    18
must be organized so that an individual model can be replaced or updated without affecting
the other prediction pipelines.

2.3.6 Portability and deployment

The entire platform must be able to run on any environment with Docker and Docker
Compose, without any manual dependency installation. Environment-specific configuration
(URLs, keys, connection parameters) must be centralized in a single .env file, kept separate
from the source code.

2.3.7 Ergonomics and usability

The interface must adapt its menus, dashboards, and available actions to the role of the
logged-in user, without exposing features beyond their scope. Information derived from the
predictive models must be presented in business language -- failure probability, remaining
useful life, parts needed -- without algorithmic terminology (model names, raw statistical
metrics). The interface must be usable from a desktop workstation as well as from a tablet
used in the field, with a layout adapted to each screen format.

2.3.8 Data integrity and confidentiality

Data for each machine must be accessible only to users whose role explicitly authorizes it;
a technician must not be able to view data for a machine that is not assigned to them.
The history of interventions, alerts, and predictions must be preserved with integrity: no

3.4 Machine Learning pipeline architecture

3.4.1 Problem framing and maintenance strategy
Industrial equipment fails along a degradation curve that is, in most cases, measurable long
before the failure itself becomes visible. Three maintenance strategies exist. Reactive
maintenance waits for failure before acting: unplanned downtime, emergency parts orders,
and production losses are its defining characteristics, and it represents the worst operating
mode in terms of total cost. Preventive maintenance acts on a fixed calendar, replacing
components that may still have significant life remaining while missing failures that occur
between scheduled intervals. Predictive maintenance is the target of this platform: it
uses sensor data and ML models to estimate when a machine will fail, which failure type is

                                                                                                                    22
developing, and what parts will be needed, and it does this before the failure occurs, so that
maintenance can be triggered at the right moment rather than too early or too late.

   The core question every component of the ML service is designed to answer is: given a
machine's current sensor readings and history, how healthy is it right now, how long before
it needs intervention, and what should the team prepare?

3.4.2 Training data: the AI4I 2020 dataset

All predictive models are initially trained on the AI4I 2020 Predictive Maintenance Dataset,
a publicly available UCI benchmark that simulates CNC machine operations. The dataset
contains 10 000 machine cycle records across 100 simulated machines, with a failure rate
of 3.4% (339 confirmed failure events distributed across five failure types). It provides the
supervised signal needed to train classification and regression models before any real machine-
specific history is available.

3.4.3 Data quality and validation methodology
Before any model is trained, four data quality constraints are enforced.

                                                                          23
Synthetic data quarantine. Every seeded or generated telemetry row is flagged is_synthetic
= true at write time. The ML service filters these rows out by default in production
(ML_ALLOW_SYNTHETIC_TELEMETRY = false), so models only consume readings that come
from real sensors. Synthetic data remains available only for local pipeline testing through an
explicit environment flag.

Null telemetry handling. If a machine has no real telemetry on record, the system does
not substitute placeholder sensor values. Calling an ML model on fabricated inputs would
produce a prediction that looks valid but is entirely meaningless. Instead, the service returns
telemetry_available: false, skips the microservice call, and falls back to a simpler rule-
based health estimate derived from maintenance history alone.

Label leakage detection. The most significant data quality issue found during develop-
ment was target leakage: the training labels for P1, P2, and P5 were generated by determin-
istic formulas of tool_wear, the same feature the models read as input. A model trained
on such data learns to reproduce the formula, not to predict real failures. These models
report near-perfect accuracy figures in validation (99% AUC-ROC) but the number mea-
sures arithmetic reproduction, not predictive power. They are tagged validation_status:
"leaked" in the model registry, and their accuracy figures are suppressed in the dashboard.
Fixing this requires replacing the formula-derived labels with real, technician-confirmed fail-
ure records.

Group-holdout validation. For time-series machine data, a random 80/20 row split is
unsound: both sets contain readings from the same machines, which means the model sees
data from a machine's own degradation trajectory in both training and test. This inflates
test scores by memorisation rather than generalisation. The correct procedure is to hold out
entire machines during training (for example, withholding machines with IDs 70­79 entirely)
so that evaluation is always performed on machines the model has never encountered. This
is the methodology used for P3, and it is the reason its validation metrics are considered
reliable while the others' are not.

field in the unified API response.

P3: Remaining Useful Life Estimation. An XGBoost regressor that estimates how

regression head trained alongside the point estimator provides a calibrated prediction interval
(P10 to P90), so the dashboard displays a range rather than a single number, reflecting the
true uncertainty in the estimate.

P4: Behavioral Anomaly Detection. A four-method ensemble that detects abnormal
sensor behaviour. Isolation Forest contributes 30% of the combined score, Z-Score normal-
ization 20%, cluster deviation 10%, and a trained neural autoencoder 40%. The ensemble
score is normalized to [0, 1]; a score above 0.5 is classified as an anomaly. Using TensorFlow
CPU, the autoencoder was trained exclusively on failure-free machine cycles and a separate
.keras artifact is loaded at startup alongside the classical methods.

P5: Work Order Priority. An XGBoost classifier that assigns a priority level (LOW,
MEDIUM, HIGH, CRITICAL) to a predicted failure, allowing the maintenance scheduler

                                                                                    25
to sequence the work queue. Like P1, its labels in the current training set are derived from
the same sensor features, making its accuracy figure unreliable until real priority judgements
from technicians are accumulated.

P6: Maintenance Scheduling. An XGBoost classifier that recommends the appropriate
scheduling window: immediate, within the week, or planned. It enables the platform to
distinguish between a machine that needs intervention today and one that can be included
in the next scheduled maintenance cycle without risk.

P7: Parts Demand Forecasting. A statistical forecasting module that predicts which
spare parts will be needed and when. It combines survival analysis (estimating the probability
that a machine will require each part within a future window) with Croston's method for
intermittent demand forecasting (suited to parts that are replaced in occasional bursts rather
than steadily). When forecast demand exceeds current stock, a shortage alert is raised and
a draft procurement request is generated for human approval.

3.4.5 Wave 2 signal processing models

A second, parallel pipeline (referred to as Wave 2) processes each machine's telemetry history
rather than a single snapshot. Four signal-processing models run concurrently:

   · a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) that encodes degradation physics
       as soft constraints in the loss function, preventing the model from producing physically
       impossible RUL estimates;

   · a Cox Proportional Hazards survival model that estimates the probability of
       failure over a future time window while explicitly handling right-censored machines
       (those still running at the observation cutoff, whose future fate is unknown);

   · a Mahalanobis Health Index that measures how far current sensor readings deviate
       from that specific machine's own healthy baseline, excluding the tool-wear reset event
       from computation to avoid false alarms immediately after a tool replacement;

   · a CUSUM anomaly detector smoothed by a Kalman filter to detect slow cumu-
       lative drift that point-in-time snapshots miss.

3.4.6 Dempster-Shafer evidence fusion

The outputs of the Wave 2 models are combined through Dempster-Shafer evidence theory
(DST), a mathematical framework for combining uncertain, potentially conflicting evidence
from independent sources. The frame of discernment is  = {Healthy, Degrading, Critical, Unknown}.

                                                                                                                    26
   Each model converts its output into a Basic Probability Assignment (BPA) over  using
piecewise-linear membership functions. A model with high confidence contributes most of
its mass to one of the three substantive hypotheses; a model that is unavailable or disagrees
strongly with the others contributes its mass to Unknown rather than distorting the combined
estimate. The combination rule switches between Dempster's rule (when conflict K  0.8)
and the more conservative Yager's rule (when K > 0.8), so that genuine disagreement between
sources is reported as uncertainty rather than silently overridden.

   This mechanism is important for the platform's credibility. A system that always produces
a confident-sounding answer, even when its own inputs contradict one another, will eventually
be confidently wrong. The DST layer is designed to say "not sure yet" explicitly, rather than
forcing a false consensus. The fused BPA yields the single unified health score (0­100) and
an overall verdict that downstream readiness and alerting logic consumes.

In everyday terms, the four boxes at the top of Figure 3.6 can be read as follows.
Drift watch keeps an eye on whether a machine's readings are slowly creeping away from

                                                                                                                28
normal, cycle after cycle. A single unusual reading is not a concern; a small change that
keeps growing, day after day, is. This is the one signal built specifically to catch that kind
of slow, easy-to-miss decline before it turns into a sudden breakdown.

   Distance from normal compares a machine's current readings against its own personal
baseline: what "normal" looks like for that specific machine, not for machines in general. The
further today's reading sits from that baseline, the more it stands out. A recent tool change
is deliberately ignored for a short while after it happens, so that a routine part swap is never
mistaken for a warning sign.

   Survival odds asks a different question: given everything known about similar machines,
how long do machines like this one typically keep running before needing attention? Impor-
tantly, it does not only look at machines that have already broken down; it also learns from
every machine that is still healthy and running today, which is what makes its estimate of
"how long is left" meaningfully more honest.

   Physics check acts as a common-sense filter on top of the other estimates. It knows
the basic physical rules a machine has to obey (for instance, that remaining lifetime cannot
suddenly jump up for no reason) and it rejects any estimate that breaks those rules, even if
a purely statistical model would have accepted it.

   Together, these four signals are combined by the fusion step: when they broadly agree,
the platform reports a confident health score; when they clearly disagree with each other, it
says so honestly instead of picking a number at random, as illustrated in the bottom half of
Figure 3.6.

3.4.7 Live inference request flow

When a user opens a machine detail page, the following sequence executes:

   1. The frontend requests GET /api/v1/ml/machines/{id}/unified-health.

   2. The backend queries PostgreSQL for the latest real sensor readings, filtering out syn-
       thetic rows. If none exist, it returns telemetry_available: false and stops here.

   3. The backend posts the sensor snapshot to the ML microservice, which runs P1­P7 in
       parallel (models are held in memory via an LRU cache after first load to avoid re-reading
       disk on every request).

   4. The Wave 2 models process the telemetry history concurrently.

   5. The DST fusion layer combines all available signals into the health score and verdict.

   6. The complete prediction record is written to ml_prediction_logs for audit and future
       retraining.

   7. The response is returned to the frontend, which renders the ML Intelligence panel.

3.4.8 Downstream processing: readiness, alerts, and procurement
The readiness score blends the unified health score (40%) with inventory coverage (30%),
procurement risk (20%), and maintenance recency (10%). Alerts aggregate signals from
both pipelines (an anomaly flag from P4, a parts shortage from P7, or a model-disagreement
flag from the fusion step itself) and a detected parts shortage triggers the purchase draft, as
shown in Figure 3.7.

            Figure 3.7: From ML outputs to readiness, alerts, and purchase drafts
   P7 acts as a smart shopping list rather than an automated buyer. Every spare part is
consumed at its own pace (some wear steadily, others are only replaced in occasional bursts)
and P7 learns that pace per part, per machine, then combines it with how likely each machine
is to need work soon. From this it estimates how many units of a given part will be needed
within the coming weeks, and compares that estimate against what is actually available in
the warehouse. When the numbers do not add up, a shortage alert is raised well before a
technician is left waiting for a part in the middle of a repair. The system never places an
order autonomously; it only prepares the suggestion, and a human must review and approve
it.

3.7 End-to-end request flow

Figure 3.1 shows the components involved; the sequence below walks through a single request,
from the user's action to the answer returned, in order:

   1. User to Backend -- a technician asks a question in the chat, or an administrator
       uploads a document. Either way, the request first lands on the FastAPI backend.

   2. Backend to RAG Service -- the backend forwards the request to the RAG microser-
       vice: an upload becomes an ingest call, a question becomes a retrieve call.

   3. RAG Service to PostgreSQL -- the RAG microservice performs its ingest/embed
       or retrieve/rerank work directly against PostgreSQL with pgvector, writing new chunks
       and vectors on ingest, reading and ranking them on retrieve.

                                                                                                                    32
   4. RAG Service to MinIO -- in parallel, the RAG microservice stores the original
       uploaded file in MinIO, or reads it back for a download.

   5. Backend to Groq LLM -- for a question, the backend takes the retrieved context
       back from the RAG service and hands it to the Groq-hosted language model.

   6. Groq LLM to User -- the language model generates the answer from that context,
       and the backend returns it to the user.

4 Implementation

4.1 Backend implementation

The backend is organized into modules by business domain (users, machines, work orders,
intervention orders, alerts, scheduling, Machine Learning). Each module exposes its API
routes, validation schemas, and business services separately, which facilitates maintenance
and future changes. Role-based access control is applied centrally on every route. The four
workflows below (maintenance planning, intervention requests, work orders, and inventory
reservations) illustrate this modular organization at the business-logic level.

4.1.1 Maintenance planning workflow
An Administrator opens a planning window in draft status; the Head Technician then breaks
it down into per-machine diagnostic or correction tasks and submits it for approval. Only
once an Administrator approves the planning are the assigned technicians notified, by both
an in-app alert and an email.

4.1.2 Intervention request workflow
A field technician's request passes through a single Head Technician validation gate with
two distinct outcomes: approval requires an associated machine and automatically creates a
linked work order, while rejection requires a reason and releases any parts reservation already
made. Closing the loop later, once the work is done, still requires a root-cause diagnosis before
the intervention can be marked validated.

4.1.3 Work order lifecycle
A work order moves through a sequential ten-state lifecycle. It can be rejected at the approval
step, releasing any linked stock reservation, and it can only be closed by an Administrator
once it has reached the validated state.

4.1.4 Inventory reservation system
A single service owns every mutation of stock quantities for the intervention/work-order work-
flow. Four independent triggers (approval, rejection, a daily scheduled task, and technician
completion) each invoke a dedicated process, all reading and writing the same reservation
records; approval handlers never touch stock tables directly.

4.2 Frontend implementation

The frontend offers three distinct application spaces, each tailored to the needs of the corre-
sponding role: an Administrator space for global management, a Head Technician space for
oversight and scheduling, and a Technician space for carrying out interventions in the field. A
consistent design system (color palette, reusable components) ensures a uniform experience
across the three spaces.

4.3 Machine Learning pipeline implementation

Each predictive model (P1 to P6) was trained and then integrated into the model-serving
microservice, with lazy, cached loading to limit response times. An explainability layer
("Why?") was added across the board: every prediction, alert, or recommendation can be
expanded into a plain-language explanation, free of Machine Learning terminology, so it
remains understandable to field technicians.

   The P7 spare parts coordination module was built in six progressive steps: a demand
forecasting engine, stock shortage alert routing, role-based interface adaptation, guided order
preparation (never automatic), an operational readiness score with an event timeline, and a
feedback loop comparing predictions against parts actually consumed.

4.4 RAG service and conversational bridge implementation

The RAG service supports importing technical documents (manuals, procedures) with ver-
sion management and cascading deletion. The bridge between the RAG service and the
ML microservice was designed to never let a conversation fail: if the ML microservice is
unavailable, a fallback estimate based on simple rules is used instead, transparently to the
user.

4.5 Pilot rollout plan (PDCA)

Deploying six new predictive models directly to every machine in the plant, with no feedback
loop, would risk both poor predictions and loss of user trust. The rollout was therefore
planned as a Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle: a controlled, measurable pilot, followed by
a formal audit (Chapter 5), before any decision to generalize.

Plan. The pilot targeted a single functional zone (surface-mount technology, a production
bottleneck with high-frequency, reliable sensor data) for eight weeks, split into three weeks
of shadow mode (predictions computed and logged, but not shown to users) followed by five
weeks of active mode, where the dashboard is revealed to two maintenance managers and
four senior technicians. Four quantitative success criteria were fixed in advance: at least
15% reduction in unexpected downtime against a three-month historical baseline; at least
80% of machines flagged critical by the failure-probability model confirmed as a real issue on
inspection; at least 75% agreement between the predicted and the technician-confirmed failure

                                                                                                                    37
type; and no more than 250 ms of additional backend latency introduced by the inference
calls.

Do. During shadow mode, every prediction was written to a dedicated log table and com-
pared, at the end of the three weeks, against the intervention orders that managers actually
created: a first check of whether the models would have caught what really happened. Dur-
ing active mode, managers were required to open an intervention order for every machine
flagged critical, and technicians were required, when closing a work order, to record the ac-
tual failure type observed. This technician-confirmed label capture is the direct precursor of
the production feedback loop described in Chapter 5: real, technician-confirmed outcomes
compared against past predictions, closing the loop between the field and the model.

4.6 Technical difficulties encountered

Several difficulties were encountered during implementation. Synchronizing trained models
between the research environment (notebooks) and the production environment (microser-
vice) required setting up a strict procedure for copying and verifying the number of features
expected by each model, in order to avoid loading a stale model incompatible with the data
format sent in production.

   Partial component availability was also a point of attention: since certain deep learning
libraries were not installed in the production environment, the anomaly module's autoencoder
was made optional, with automatic renormalization of the detection ensemble's weights when
this component is absent. Likewise, the ML-RAG bridge was designed to fall back to a simple
rule-based estimate if the ML microservice is unavailable, ensuring a response is always
provided to the user.

   Finally, the consistency of large-scale test data (several thousand simulated records) re-
quired systematic verification of the representativeness of the generated failure scenarios, so
that evaluation metrics would reflect realistic cases.

5 Testing and Results

5.1 Test strategy

System validation relies on two levels of testing: unit tests verifying the isolated behavior
of each business service, and integration tests verifying the end-to-end behavior of the main
user journeys (creating a work order, raising an alert, the conversational assistant's response).
The test suite covers 99 tests in total (46 unit and core tests, 53 backend integration tests),
all passing against the live Docker stack.

5.2 Machine Learning model evaluation

5.2.1 Validation methodology
Evaluating ML models on industrial time-series data requires care. The naive approach
(splitting rows randomly into a training set and a test set) is unsound for machine lifecycle
data: since both sets contain readings from the same physical machines, the model effectively
sees parts of a machine's degradation trajectory in training and is then tested on adjacent
readings from that same trajectory. This inflates test scores by memorisation rather than
genuine generalisation.

   The correct methodology for this data type is group-holdout validation: entire ma-
chines are withheld from training (for example, machines 70­79), so that every evaluation
metric reflects performance on machines the model has never encountered during training.
This is the methodology applied to P3, and it is the reason its evaluation figures are consid-
ered reliable. For all other models, the validation methodology was not yet applied at the
time of writing due to the label quality issues described below.

5.2.2 Label quality and validation status
During development, a systematic audit of the training labels for all seven models identified
a critical data quality issue: the labels used to train P1, P2, and P5 were generated by
deterministic formulas of tool_wear, the same feature these models read as input. This
constitutes target leakage, also called label tautology: the model learns to reproduce the
formula rather than to predict real failures. No amount of additional synthetic data, feature

                                                          41
engineering, or algorithm changes can correct this; it is a problem with the label source, not
the model.

   The consequence is that accuracy figures for P1, P2, and P5 (approximately 99% AUC-
ROC under group-holdout) measure how faithfully the model reproduces an arithmetic iden-
tity, not how well it predicts real machine failures. Displaying these numbers as performance
metrics would mislead stakeholders into trusting predictions that have no validated relation-
ship to real failure events. For this reason, the model registry assigns each model an explicit
validation_status field, and the dashboard suppresses accuracy figures for models tagged
as "leaked".

5.2.4 Retraining strategy

A production gate is enforced on every retrain: a new model artifact is only written to disk
if it meets or exceeds the minimum threshold for its metric. This prevents a bad retrain from
silently overwriting a good model.

   The trigger strategy varies by model and depends on its validation status:

   · P1 and P5 are frozen until real technician-confirmed failure records replace the formula-
       derived labels. Retraining on more synthetic data would only reinforce the tautology.

   · P2 is retrained on a manual trigger when a meaningful batch of new technician-
       confirmed failure-type labels accumulates, using a repeated group-holdout cross-validation
       pass to report mean and standard deviation of macro-F1 across folds.

   · P3 is the only model scheduled for a regular cadence: monthly batch retraining, or
       triggered whenever a full machine lifecycle newly closes out and becomes available as
       a training sample. It is first in line for new regressor accuracy gating because it is the
       most trustworthy model in the pipeline.

   · P4, P6, and P7 are retrained on an event-driven basis when sufficient real outcome
       data (confirmed anomaly flags, intervention outcomes, actual parts consumption) has
       accumulated to constitute a meaningful update signal.

5.2.5 What real data would unlock

The current platform is built on a solid architecture but is limited by the quality of available
training data. Three concrete investments would change the situation significantly.

                                                                                  43
   First, technician-confirmed failure records: when a technician closes a work order
and records the root cause (TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF, or RNF), that observation is a label
P1 and P2 can genuinely learn from. Accumulating 1 000 or more confirmed events over six
to twelve months is the minimum threshold for meaningful retraining.

   Second, real run-to-failure sequences for P3: tracking fifteen to twenty machines
continuously from healthy state to confirmed failure (or documented decommission) would
provide P3 with real censored sequences, enabling a migration to Cox Proportional Hazards
or Weibull AFT as the production model.

   Third, vibration and motor current sensors: the five sensor inputs currently available
cannot capture bearing degradation, the most common industrial failure mode. Vibration
RMS and kurtosis, together with motor current draw, are the signals that would make P1,
P3, and P4 substantively more powerful, because they provide information that is genuinely
independent of tool wear.

5.3 Pilot audit (PDCA Check/Act)

Following the Plan and Do phases of the pilot rollout described in Chapter 4, the Check and
Act phases close the PDCA cycle with a formal audit and a decision on how to proceed.

Check. The quantitative audit computed a confusion matrix on the failure-probability
model, paying particular attention to false positives (an over-eager model that triggers un-
necessary inspections erodes trust, alert fatigue) and false negatives (undetected failures,
which must be traced back through the telemetry to understand what the model missed),
alongside the mean absolute error of the remaining-useful-life estimate. The qualitative audit
consisted of structured debrief interviews with the pilot technicians on whether the predicted
priorities matched their own judgement of urgency, and on the clarity of the dashboard.

Act. Two outcomes were planned for. If the models underperformed (excessive false pos-
itives or false negatives), the response was to collect the newly accumulated, technician-
confirmed data, retrain on it, and simplify the interface if it had caused confusion, opening
a new, shorter PDCA cycle. If the pilot met its targets, the response was to standardize the
dashboard across every production zone, to let the system automatically generate a draft in-
tervention order (pending human validation) whenever failure probability and anomaly score
both cross a high-confidence threshold, and to update the formal maintenance procedure
documentation accordingly.

   The guarded, human-in-the-loop automation envisioned in this last step (the system pro-
poses, a person approves) is the same governing principle already applied to the production
procurement-draft workflow (Chapter 3) and to the shadow prediction log used for audit-
ing and retraining above: every automated suggestion in the platform stops short of an

                                                                                                                    44
autonomous action.

5.4 Use case demonstrations

Three scenarios illustrate the end-to-end operation of the platform:
   · Creating and tracking an intervention order: a technician receives an intervention
       order assigned by the Head Technician, views it from their space, and then closes it
       once completed, which updates the history of the machine concerned;
   · Receiving a stock shortage alert: when the P7 module detects that a part needed

       and presented to the Head Technician along with an order proposal for validation;
   · Querying the conversational assistant: a technician asks the assistant about the
       troubleshooting procedure for a specific machine; the response combines the technical
       documentation with the machine's real-time health status.

5.5 Difficulties encountered during testing

The testing phase highlighted the importance of training data representativeness: the models
were validated on a historical dataset simulating several dozen complete maintenance cycles,
in order to cover a sufficient diversity of failure scenarios. The computation time of cer-
tain models (in particular the hybrid remaining-useful-life model) required optimizing model
loading, which is cached in memory to avoid reloading from disk on every request.

   A subtler difficulty involved the interaction between synthetic telemetry and real-time
inference. When the platform was seeded with generated readings flagged is_synthetic
= true, every machine displayed identical sensor values and ML outputs, because the ML
service was silently substituting fixed placeholder values when no real readings passed the syn-
thetic filter. The fix replaced silent placeholder defaults with an explicit telemetry_available:
false flag, stopping the model call entirely when no real data exists and making the absence
of data visible to the user rather than hidden behind a plausible-looking number.

General Conclusion

This end-of-studies project made it possible to design and implement an asset management
and predictive maintenance platform for industrial equipment, addressing the problem stated
in the introduction: unifying, within a single system, traditional operational asset man-
agement, machine-learning-based failure prediction capabilities, and intelligent support for
maintenance teams.

   The approach followed -- requirements analysis, design of a modular architecture, iterative
implementation, then validation through testing -- made it possible to deliver a coherent
system in which each Machine Learning module (P1 to P7) is seamlessly integrated into the
day-to-day management of work and intervention orders, with systematic explainability to
remain accessible to non-specialist users.

   On the technical side, this internship deepened my understanding of microservices architec-
ture design, the application of Machine Learning techniques to concrete industrial problems
(classification, regression on survival data, anomaly detection, fusion of multiple sources of
evidence), as well as the implementation of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems.
On a personal level, this internship strengthened my autonomy in running a project end to
end, my ability to document and justify technical choices, and my rigor in systematically
validating each component before integrating it.

   Several avenues for improvement emerge from this work: extending the RAG service to
new document formats, training the models on real production data rather than simulated
data, and strengthening human oversight in the automation of spare parts provisioning.

A.1 Selected code excerpts

The excerpt below illustrates, in simplified form, the score fusion logic of the anomaly detec-
tion ensemble (P4 module), with weight renormalization when a detector is unavailable:

def fuse_anomaly_scores(scores: dict, weights: dict) -> float:
      available = {k: v for k, v in scores.items() if v is not None}
      total_weight = sum(weights[k] for k in available)
      if total_weight == 0:
            return 0.0
      return sum(scores[k] * weights[k] for k in available) / total_weight

   The following excerpt illustrates the principle behind computing the operational readiness
score (P7 module), combining several weighted signals:

def compute_readiness_score(health, inventory, shortage, recency):
      return (
            0.40 * health
            + 0.30 * inventory
            - 0.20 * shortage
            + 0.10 * recency
      )
