\chapter*{General Conclusion}
\markboth{General Conclusion}{}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Conclusion}

The introduction asked whether a single system could hold the operational plumbing of industrial asset management (users, machines, work orders, interventions) together with a genuine predictive layer, without one dragging the other down. It held: the seven ML modules (P1 to P7) sit inside the same work-order and intervention-order lifecycle a technician already uses, not bolted alongside it, and every one of their outputs ships with a plain-language explanation rather than a raw model score, which was the condition for the platform being trusted enough to actually use. Getting there followed a deliberately unglamorous path, requirements first, then a modular architecture, then implementation in slices, then testing against what the requirements actually demanded, rather than jumping straight to the models. That order is what kept the ML layer from becoming a demo bolted onto a working EAM system instead of a genuine part of it.

Technically, the internship forced a real reckoning with microservices boundaries, with applying ML to problems that do not resemble a textbook dataset (survival-based regression, anomaly detection, reconciling several disagreeing evidence sources), and with building a Retrieval-Augmented Generation pipeline end to end rather than wiring up an existing one. Less technically, it meant owning a six-month scope alone: deciding what to build next, writing down why, and refusing to call a component done until it had actually been checked against real behaviour rather than assumed to work. What is left undone says as much as what shipped: the RAG service should read more formats, the models need real technician-confirmed outcomes, and the spare-parts automation should stay exactly as human-gated as it is now, or more so, not less, as it matures.
